The Paris Syndrome of the Eastern European

There is a particular kind of fever that can attack an Eastern European somewhere between the périphérique and the Carrefour parking lot.

It begins innocently.

You arrive in France carrying an enormous invisible suitcase packed with books.

There is Camus in the side pocket. Debord is sitting heavily on top of him. Baudrillard is wedged between two shirts. Barthes is somewhere underneath the socks. Lefebvre has been folded badly and refuses to straighten himself. Sartre is smoking in the luggage compartment. Bourdieu has somehow acquired the passport.

You have been told, directly or indirectly, that France is a country in which ideas have escaped from the university and contaminated everyday life.

You imagine cafés where somebody has spent three hours arguing about the ontology of freedom.

You imagine provincial streets designed by people who have read Proust.

You imagine citizens who know what the Republic means, workers who understand alienation, intellectuals who can explain the spectacle, pensioners who can distinguish between civilisation and mere consumption.

You imagine a country in which philosophy is not simply a school subject but a subterranean operating system.

Then you arrive.

And there is the Lidl.

It is enormous.

You stare at it.

There is a parking lot larger than your village.

There are forty-seven cars idling around it.

Someone is sitting alone in an SUV watching TikTok.

A Renault Captur is waiting seventeen metres from the entrance because apparently seventeen metres is now an unreasonable walking distance.

There is an enormous illuminated sign advertising four flavours of industrial yoghurt.

And suddenly you understand that you have been the victim of a literary fraud.

Not a fraud committed by France.

A fraud committed by your own imagination.

Because the France of the books is real.

That is precisely the problem.

The philosophical France was not imaginary. It produced a gigantic body of thought about modernity, alienation, signs, power, consumption, urban space, freedom, desire and the spectacle.

And then, with almost comic precision, the country went ahead and built the thing those philosophers were describing.

This is where the Eastern European experiences the peculiar French form of culture shock.

The shock is not:

“France is not sophisticated.”

It is:

“France is sophisticated enough to have theorised its own absurdity and then commercialised it.”

---

Debord becomes particularly difficult to read in a supermarket.

The spectacle, you discover, does not need a dictator.

It needs a loyalty card.

It does not necessarily announce itself through propaganda.

It whispers:

“2+1 gratuit.”

It does not tell you what to think.

It tells you what to buy.

And the genius of the arrangement is that nobody feels oppressed.

Everybody is merely choosing.

Choose your yoghurt.

Choose your insurance.

Choose your motorway subscription.

Choose your streaming platform.

Choose your car.

Choose your profile picture.

Choose your algorithm.

Choose your supermarket.

Choose your political identity from twelve pre-packaged narratives.

Choose your coffee machine.

Choose your emotional state from an application.

Choose your lifestyle.

Choose between 4G and 5G.

Choose whether you want the 128 GB or 256 GB version of the device that will spend the next four years choosing things for you.

The spectacle has learned an extraordinary trick.

It no longer needs to tell you what reality is.

It gives you a menu.

---

And then there is the car.

The car is perhaps the most beautiful demonstration of the gap between French intellectual history and French material life.

France produced Lefebvre's investigations of everyday life.

And then it produced the zone commerciale.

France theorised urban alienation.

And then it made you drive twelve kilometres to buy toothpaste.

France had philosophers worrying about the domination of everyday existence.

And then it built a roundabout.

Another roundabout.

A larger roundabout.

A retail park.

A bypass.

A petrol station.

Another retail park.

A discount furniture warehouse.

A McDonald's.

An access road.

A second access road because apparently the first access road was insufficiently automobile-compatible.

The European visitor from the East begins to suspect that the real French philosophical object is not the text.

It is the road network.

The road tells the truth.

The book says: the everyday is colonised by abstract systems.

The road says: yes.

The book says: space is socially produced.

The parking lot says: watch this.

The book says: modernity restructures human relations.

The supermarket says: please scan your fidélité card.

Suddenly Baudrillard makes sense.

Not intellectually.

Geometrically.

---

And this is where the Eastern European becomes slightly confused.

Because back home, especially for somebody who grew up with the cultural memory of late socialism, consumerism initially appeared almost glamorous.

The West was associated with abundance.

A Western supermarket was practically a metaphysical object.

There were bananas.

There were six kinds of cheese.

There were detergents with mysterious names.

There were cars without rust.

There were advertisements.

There was freedom.

Consumer abundance appeared as the opposite of the grey, administratively constrained world.

So the young Eastern European learned an equation:

wealth = consumer choice.

And then they arrive in France and encounter a strange inversion.

The abundance is real.

The freedom is more complicated.

There are hundreds of products.

But the landscape increasingly exists to deliver those products to you efficiently.

The city becomes a logistics problem.

The village becomes a parking problem.

The human body becomes a transport problem.

The evening becomes a screen.

The weekend becomes a shopping ritual.

And the citizen slowly mutates into a customer.

Not because somebody ordered the transformation.

Because the infrastructure makes it convenient.

---

This is perhaps the most disturbing aspect of the whole thing.

Nobody has abolished spiritual life.

It has simply been outcompeted.

There is no minister of secularisation.

There is no ministry of shopping malls.

There is no authoritarian committee deciding that the philosophical tradition must disappear.

The replacement is much softer.

It is Friday evening.

You are tired.

You have spent the day working.

You open your phone.

There are videos.

There are messages.

There are advertisements.

There are people you vaguely know.

There is a new restaurant.

There is a flash sale.

There is a new phone.

There is a property listing.

There is political outrage.

There is an influencer explaining how to optimise your morning routine.

There is a man demonstrating a pressure washer.

There is a woman reviewing a vacuum cleaner.

There is a video of somebody restoring an old Peugeot.

There are seventeen notifications.

You look up.

It is midnight.

Nothing terrible happened.

Nothing illegal happened.

Nobody forced you.

You simply did not live.

You consumed a continuous stream of representations of life.

This is the truly strange successor to the old spectacle.

The spectacle no longer merely shows you images of the world.

It prevents you from noticing that you have spent the entire evening looking at images instead of inhabiting your own existence.

---

The Eastern European has another source of confusion.

In the East, there is often still a memory of scarcity.

The grandmother kept things.

The grandfather repaired things.

The family knew somebody who could weld something.

A chair could be fixed.

A radio could be opened.

A machine could be understood.

A car could be maintained indefinitely out of spite.

There was a culture of improvisation, sometimes born from poverty and sometimes from necessity.

And then the French consumer economy introduces a different metaphysics:

the object has an expected lifespan.

Not necessarily because it has become physically impossible to repair.

Because the economic ecosystem around it has become hostile to repair.

The appliance is obsolete.

The software is obsolete.

The interface is obsolete.

The subscription is obsolete.

The battery is inaccessible.

The replacement part is unavailable.

The new model has arrived.

The old model still works perfectly.

This is a peculiar kind of planned psychological depreciation.

The object has not died.

Your relationship with it has been declared outdated.

And somehow this is called progress.

---

The tragedy becomes almost comic when one notices how much French cultural mythology depends upon resisting exactly this tendency.

France possesses one of the great traditions of skepticism toward bourgeois normality.

It has produced entire intellectual industries dedicated to asking:

What is behind the appearance?

Who constructed this social reality?

Who benefits from this structure?

What does this symbol signify?

How is desire manufactured?

How does everyday life become administered?

How does power become internalised?

How does consumption become identity?

Why does the modern individual confuse freedom with choice?

These are not obscure questions in the history of French thought.

And yet the contemporary citizen can leave a university seminar discussing alienation, get into a financed Renault, drive to a shopping centre, buy a smart television, return home, spend three hours on Instagram and order dinner from an application.

The contradiction is so complete that it almost becomes a parody of theory.

The society has not refuted the philosophers.

It has demonstrated them.

---

This is why the Eastern European version of Paris Syndrome can become much darker than ordinary tourist disappointment.

The tourist expects beauty and discovers bureaucracy.

The Eastern European expects philosophy and discovers merchandising.

They have not merely arrived in a country.

They have arrived inside a cultural quotation.

Except the quotation has been removed from quotation marks.

You read Debord.

Then you drive on the A6.

You read Baudrillard.

Then you enter a shopping centre.

You read Barthes.

Then you see the advertising.

You read Lefebvre.

Then you walk through a city whose edges have dissolved into commercial infrastructure.

You read Camus.

Then you spend twenty minutes looking for parking.

At some point you begin to suspect that the great French philosophical tradition is not describing a society.

It is describing a haunted house.

And you are now inside it.

---

The weirdest part is that Americanisation does not necessarily arrive in an American form.

It arrives wearing French clothes.

The shopping centre may have a French logo.

The car may be French.

The café may be French.

The supermarket chain may be French.

The television may be French.

The bureaucracy may remain spectacularly French.

But underneath the national costumes there is an increasingly universal grammar:

mobility, consumption, branding, individualisation, entertainment, algorithmic attention, private comfort.

The Americanisation is therefore not simply the appearance of American products.

It is the dominance of a particular idea of life.

Life becomes a sequence of consumer-mediated choices.

The good life becomes better purchasing power.

Freedom becomes mobility.

Mobility becomes car ownership.

Sociality becomes platforms.

Identity becomes profile.

Culture becomes content.

Leisure becomes consumption.

Nature becomes scenery.

The citizen becomes a demographic segment.

The village becomes an address.

The city becomes an access network.

And the philosophical tradition becomes something you studied for the baccalauréat.

---

Perhaps this is why the Eastern European occasionally develops an absurd affection for the ugly parts of France.

The old village café.

The cracked municipal building.

The abandoned railway station.

The tiny bakery.

The neglected cemetery.

The old plane trees beside a road.

The workshop where somebody has repaired the same machine for thirty years.

The battered Peugeot that refuses to die.

The eccentric public bench.

The old stone wall.

The bizarre municipal noticeboard covered in faded paper.

These things feel strangely alive.

Not because they are picturesque.

Because they are not perfectly optimised.

They contain evidence of human time.

Their inefficiency is almost spiritual.

A perfectly engineered commercial landscape tells you what to do.

A decaying provincial street asks you what happened here.

And perhaps that is why the Eastern European keeps looking for France in the places where France seems least successful as a consumer product.

There, among the weeds, broken shutters, empty workshops and obsolete infrastructure, something remains.

Not prosperity.

Not glamour.

Something more dangerous.

History.

---

The Paris Syndrome eventually changes character.

At first it is disappointment.

Then irritation.

Then irony.

Then recognition.

Because eventually you realise the problem was never that France failed to become the France of the philosophers.

The problem is that the philosophers were frighteningly accurate.

The shopping mall did not defeat philosophy.

It became philosophy's field experiment.

The car did not destroy the city by accident.

It reorganised the meaning of distance.

The smartphone did not merely create distraction.

It transformed attention into territory.

The supermarket did not merely sell commodities.

It turned everyday existence into a continuous interface with the market.

And the Eastern European, who arrived expecting to discover the land of Camus, discovers something more uncomfortable:

Camus is still there.

Debord is still there.

Baudrillard is still there.

Lefebvre is still there.

They are standing silently beside the parking lot.

They are looking at the giant illuminated sign.

They are watching somebody unlock their car with an app.

And they are probably wondering why anybody is still surprised.

---

Perhaps the real cultural shock is therefore not discovering that France became Americanised.

It is discovering that modernity won without having to declare victory.

Nobody rang the bell.

Nobody announced the end of philosophy.

Nobody abolished the café.

Nobody banned metaphysics.

They simply built a retail park across the road.

And another one.

And another.

Until, one evening, you look around and notice that the old spiritual architecture of life has been replaced by an architecture of access:

access to products,

access to transport,

access to entertainment,

access to platforms,

access to credit,

access to infinitely renewable distraction.

Everything is accessible.

And increasingly nothing is inhabitable.

That is the final form of the Eastern European Paris Syndrome.

You came to France expecting a civilisation obsessed with the question:

“How should one live?”

And you discover a civilisation increasingly organised around the much easier question:

“What are you buying?”

The unsettling thing is not that the second question exists.

It is that the first one has become so difficult to hear.
